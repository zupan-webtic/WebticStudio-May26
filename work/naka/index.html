A complex product, a hard deadline, and a website that had to do the explaining.

Naka website — homepage, mission, and infrastructure overview

01 The brief

Naka came to us two months before a major international conference. Their current website was not ready to be seen by investors, press, and potential partners — and they knew it.

The design had drifted from their brand identity. The copy they had was dense, hard to follow, and not written for the web. The structure treated every visitor the same, regardless of who they were or what they were looking for. And throughout the site, animations played on repeat without explaining anything.

They had the content. They had the product. They needed someone to make sense of it all — and build a site around it that could hold its own on a conference stage.

That is what we were brought in to do.

02 What we were working with

The Naka team handed us their copy with one honest admission: they were not happy with it themselves.

It was technically accurate, but it was not built for a website. Key messages were buried. The language assumed too much prior knowledge. Different sections contradicted each other in tone. And the product — which is genuinely complex — had no clear narrative thread pulling a reader through it.

On the design side, we had no direction from the client at all. The visual language was entirely our call. The only reference point was a single sentence from the CEO: he liked Stripe.

We took that and started from scratch.

03 How we work

Before a single pixel gets placed, we need to know how a client sees the world. Not what they say they want — what they actually respond to.

We started by sending Naka five reference websites and asking them to grade each one from one to ten. No briefs, no explanations — just a number. Those numbers told us more about their instincts than any mood board conversation would have. Based on their responses, we sent ten more. By the end of that exercise, we had a precise read on their visual sensibility: what felt right, what felt wrong, and where the line sat between the two. That became our creative compass for everything that followed.

With direction established, we moved to the homepage wireframe. We presented three distinct structural concepts — three genuinely different ways to open the Naka story. The team chose one. Each concept reflected a different strategic decision about hierarchy, audience, and narrative order. Choosing between them was a meaningful decision, and Naka made it clearly.

From there, the process was sequential and deliberately tight. Each page was designed, reviewed, and confirmed before it moved into development — no parallel tracks, no going back. Once a page was signed off, it was built. That discipline kept the project from accumulating debt that would have been impossible to clear in an eight-week window.

Animations followed the same logic. We presented a concept and a few approaches to motion — different levels of complexity, different relationships between movement and content. Naka picked a direction. We built every animation in the site from that single approved concept, which meant nothing felt inconsistent or bolted on after the fact.

After development, we ran full bug testing across devices and browsers, collected final feedback, and worked through a structured round of fixes before sign-off. The process from brief to launch was clean — one direction, moved through in order, with decisions made and held.

Then came the legal hub.

04 How we approached the content

Copy first, structure second, design third.

Before we touched a layout, we worked through Naka's content to understand what they actually needed to communicate, and to whom. Their audience is not one type of person — it includes fintech companies wanting to build on their infrastructure, merchants evaluating payment solutions, and partners assessing compliance requirements. The old site treated them all the same. We separated the narrative paths so each visitor could find their way in quickly.

Once the content had a logic to it, we built the structure around that logic. Then the design came last — not as decoration, but as the delivery mechanism for what the copy and structure had already established.

Animations that explain, not decorate.

The previous site used motion as atmosphere. We rebuilt every animation from a single question: what does this explain? The result was a set of sequences that walk visitors through Naka's product layer by layer — how their infrastructure works, how it integrates with an existing client's systems, what their network supports. Visitors who would never read a technical doc can now understand the product by watching the page.

A visual identity brought back to life.

The old site had almost entirely abandoned Naka's brand green. Everything was grey. We reintroduced the brand colour as a precise accent — not overwhelming, but unmistakably present on the elements that mattered. The difference between the two sites, visually, is the difference between a company that does not know who it is and one that does.

A legal hub built for hundreds of documents.

After the main site was complete, Naka came to us with an additional request: a full legal documentation hub. It was a workstream with no template to follow. Naka operates under regulatory frameworks in three jurisdictions and holds ISO certifications that require legal documents to be continuously accessible. We designed and built a custom interface that organises roughly 300 documents by entity, type, and jurisdiction — navigable in seconds, and structured to signal compliance credibility to partners and regulators at a glance.

Naka legal hub — entity documents and document viewer

05 When it got hard

The first launch had a problem. The legal document cross-referencing was broken — links were not routing correctly, and under Naka's ISO certification terms, legal documentation cannot be inaccessible for more than six hours.

We made the call to revert to the old site before the window closed and rebuild the affected architecture before going live again.

A few days later, we launched for the second time. It has been live without incident since.

Every agency looks capable when everything goes to plan. What actually tells you something about a team is how they respond when it does not.

We kept Naka's certification intact, communicated clearly, fixed the problem properly, and delivered.

06 The result

Naka debuted the site at their conference. It performed exactly as it needed to — clear enough for a first-time visitor, credible enough for a seasoned investor, and detailed enough for a technical partner doing due diligence.

The feedback from the Naka team was the kind that does not come easily from a demanding client. The site has since drawn consistent unsolicited praise from people across the industry — not because of how it looks, though it looks good, but because of how well it works.
